# Vowel compromises

Every Russglish word has to be pronounceable as one thing by two sets of ears.
The consonants mostly take care of themselves — where they disagree there is an
engineered compromise phone (`ipa.ts`: `t̪`, `s̺`, `kx`, `ɟ`, `ɦ`, `t̪θ`) and the
choice is made once, in the inventory.

The vowels are decided per word, and this is the record of how.

Each rule names what ENGLISH has and what RUSSIAN has, and what Russglish takes.
The neutral letter is in brackets.

---

## 1. English /aɪ/ against Russian /i/ → **/əi/** `Y`

The English long-⟨i⟩ words. Russian borrowed them with a plain /i/; Russglish
keeps the diphthong, because an English reader who sees ⟨i⟩ or ⟨y⟩ in a stressed
open syllable will say /aɪ/ whatever the lexicon claims.

|            | English          | Russian         | Russglish   |
| ---------- | ---------------- | --------------- | ----------- |
| `HYbrId`   | h**y**brid /aɪ/  | г**и**брид /i/  | ḥ**ỵ**brid  |
| `dYal'ekt` | d**i**alect /aɪ/ | д**и**алект /i/ | d**i**a̱léct |
| `swYn`     | sw**i**ne /aɪ/   | св**и**нья /i/  | sw**i**ne   |

Both columns usually come out cheaper than the alternative: ⟨swine⟩ needs no mark
at all, and Russian ⟨и̱⟩ already spells /əi/.

## 2. English /ɪ/ against Russian /i/ → **/ɪ/** `I`

It sounds better to an English speaker and the difference is imperceptible to a
Russian one, whose language has no /ɪ/ to contrast it with.

|            | English          | Russian         | Russglish    |
| ---------- | ---------------- | --------------- | ------------ |
| `mIni`     | m**i**ni /ɪ/     | м**и**ни /i/    | m**ị**ni̱     |
| `hulIg'an` | hool**i**gan /ɪ/ | хул**и**ган /i/ | hool**ị**gá̱n |
| `HYbrId`   | hybr**i**d /ɪ/   | гибр**и**д /i/  | ḥỵbr**i**d   |
| `kreAt'Iv` | creat**i**ve /ɪ/ | креат**и**в /i/ | crẹạt**ị́**ve |

**Exception**: `inglIX` takes /i/ in the first syllable, by ear. It also buys
back both spellings — ⟨Ẹnglish⟩ is the real English word, and Russian needs no
mark until the second vowel.

## 3. English /eɪ/ or /e/ against Russian /a/ → **/æ/** `A`

/æ/ sits between them, and this is the commonest compromise in the lexicon.

|             | English             | Russian           | Russglish      |
| ----------- | ------------------- | ----------------- | -------------- |
| `-At`       | -**a**te /eɪ/       | -**а**ть /a/      | -**ạ**te       |
| `KAos`      | ch**a**os /eɪ/      | х**а**ос /a/      | kh**ạ**os      |
| `deklAr'At` | decl**a**re /e/     | декл**а**р- /a/   | decl**ạ**rạ́te  |
| `kreAt'Iv`  | cre**a**tive /eɪ/   | кре**а**тив /a/   | crẹ**ạ**tị́ve   |
| `derivAciE` | deriv**a**tion /eɪ/ | дерив**а**ция /a/ | dẹri̱v**ạ**tion |

## 4. English /æ/ against Russian /a/ → **case by case**, default /a/

NOT a rule. Take /æ/ only where the word as a whole already leans Russian —
its stress, its ending — and wants a nudge back towards English. Otherwise /a/.

|              |     |                                                                        |
| ------------ | --- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `Atmosfera`  | /æ/ | Russian stress AND Russian -a ending; the one free vowel leans English |
| `planet`     | /a/ | left alone                                                             |
| `stand'ard`  | /a/ | left alone                                                             |
| `sem'antika` | /a/ | left alone                                                             |

## 5. English schwa against Russian /a/ → **/a/**

Never /æ/. An unstressed English vowel has no quality worth defending.

|            | English          | Russian         | Russglish    |
| ---------- | ---------------- | --------------- | ------------ |
| `dYal'ekt` | di**a**lect /ə/  | ди**а**лект /a/ | di**a̱**léct  |
| `hulIg'an` | hoolig**a**n /ə/ | хулиг**а**н /a/ | hoolị́g**á̱**n |

## Consonants: no final devoicing

Russian devoices its own final obstruents; Russglish does not inherit the rule.
`peri'od` is /periˈod/ and `HYbrId` ends /d/, because an English reader would
trip over ⟨periot⟩ while a Russian ear — which devoices finals anyway — hears
⟨перио́д⟩ either way. Where that forces a choice, the cost goes to the Russian
column: `stand'ard` is spelled ⟨станда́рд⟩ though Russian writes стандарт.

## Stress

The default is penultimate (`placeStress`), and a `'` in the neutral form marks
an exception. Two conventions:

- **Russian stress wins** for the Greek/Latin international stratum — лексико́н,
  компоне́нт, семáнтика, хулигáн — since those words are stressed alike in
  neither language and Russian is the more consistent guide.
- **`-At` takes the stress** of any verb it builds: `kreAtIv'At`, `levIt'At`,
  `deklAr'At`. Left unstressed the verb sounds like its root with something
  trailing off the end.

Where the shape of a word is already Russian, the stress may lean English to
balance it — `woDa` is /ˈwot̪a/ (WAter) not /woˈt̪a/ (вода́).
